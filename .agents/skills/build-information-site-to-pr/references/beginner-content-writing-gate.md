## 1. Core rule

Write the page to help a newcomer understand the subject, not to demonstrate
the writer's caution or train the reader in source criticism.

Use this order:

```text
begin with a concrete question the reader can recognize
→ introduce the object, person, place, or event
→ supply only the prerequisite knowledge needed next
→ say what happened
→ explain how it works
→ show the result with concrete examples
→ explain why it matters
→ offer clearly named sources for further reading
```

Prefer concrete nouns, active verbs, short causal explanations, and familiar
sentence structures.

Begin from a question that a member of the public can understand before knowing
the specialist vocabulary. Do not begin from an abstract verdict, theoretical
framework, scholarly dispute, or visual metaphor. The opening must name the
subject and let the reader know what they are about to encounter.

Do not make the reader decode:

- the writer's research process;
- the distinction between every source and inference;
- repeated warnings about what cannot be proven;
- abstract analytical vocabulary before the object is explained.

## 2. Consolidate evidence boundaries

An information page may include one reader-facing section or callout titled
`Evidence and boundaries` when it helps the reader understand a meaningful
dispute, uncertainty, missing record, reconstruction, or interpretive limit.

Place it after the complete main explanation, not between ordinary facts. It is
supplementary and does not count as a fourth major explanatory chapter in a
short three-part entry.

Use one consolidated note, not a chain of warnings. Do not append boilerplate
after every sentence or paragraph such as:

> The source confirms the exhibition and dates, but this site's structural
> interpretation is not the work itself or the only possible conclusion.

Do not label every statement that is less than 100 percent certain as "not a
historical fact," "only an interpretation," or "cannot be proved." Ordinary
historical and cultural writing often contains attributed interpretation,
approximate dates, incomplete records, or reasonable synthesis. Handle those
with normal prose.

Choose among three levels:

1. **No public boundary note:** use when direct facts and ordinary attribution
   are enough.
2. **One short closing note:** use when the reader should know one material
   limitation, but it is not the subject of the page.
3. **One developed closing section:** use only when the dispute, missing
   evidence, contested identity, reconstruction, or competing interpretation is
   itself important enough to explain.

Within the main explanation:

- state supported facts plainly;
- attribute a person's or institution's intention directly, for example
  `主办方介绍……`;
- use local wording such as `约`, `目前已知`, `主办方称`, or `学者通常认为`
  only where it prevents a materially false statement;
- omit speculation that adds no useful explanation;
- keep internal confidence labels and research notes out of public prose.

Do not turn one necessary qualifier into a general disclaimer paragraph.

## 3. Use three parts for short entries

For a short exhibition, artwork, event, news item, object, or concept, default
to no more than three major explanatory parts:

### 1. What is it?

Answer:

- what happened or what the object is;
- who made, organized, or experienced it;
- where and when;
- the one fact needed to orient a newcomer.

### 2. How does it work?

Answer:

- what it is made from;
- what the visitor sees or does;
- the sequence, mechanism, relationship, or comparison;
- the few details needed to understand the form.

### 3. Why is it worth attention?

Answer:

- what becomes newly visible;
- why the material, choice, relationship, or timing matters;
- one concrete question or takeaway the visitor can carry forward.

Sources and, when genuinely useful, one consolidated evidence-boundary note may
follow the three parts. Do not count either as another major explanatory
chapter.

Use more than three sections only when the material genuinely requires a longer
journey. Do not split one short idea into "Context," "Why," "Evidence,"
"Analysis," and "Boundary" merely to make the page look methodical.

For a genuinely long article, course, experiment, historical sequence, or
technical explanation, expand the same learning order rather than forcing it
into three headings:

```text
familiar entry question
→ object introduction
→ minimum prerequisite knowledge
→ process
→ result and examples
→ cause and meaning
→ one consolidated dispute or boundary section when needed
→ reflection or application at a difficulty taught by the page
```

Beginner-friendly does not mean deleting information. Retain names, dates,
terms, and numbers, then restore the intermediate steps that specialist writing
often omits.

When presenting a derived number:

1. name each input;
2. show how the inputs combine;
3. state the total;
4. explain why the total matters.

When presenting a comparison or analytical dimension, provide the actual
result. Do not merely say that differences involve material, length, status, or
decoration; tell the reader what each compared group or object actually shows.

## 4. Write sentences a newcomer can paraphrase

Every sentence must pass:

> Can someone unfamiliar with the subject restate this sentence after reading
> it once?

Require each sentence to make clear:

- who or what is acting;
- what happened;
- where and when when relevant;
- what unfamiliar term refers to;
- why the sentence follows the previous one.

Prefer one main job per sentence.

Replace:

- abstract nouns with the object or action they describe;
- long chains joined by "and," "while," "coincides," or semicolons with a
  short sequence;
- pronouns whose referent is not obvious;
- phrases such as "structural reading," "infrastructure of unity," or
  "material lineage" with visible actions and relationships.

Avoid sentences that combine an anniversary, a venue, another group of works,
and an art-historical conclusion without explaining how they connect.

## 5. Explain dates, causes, and relationships

Do not write:

> The exhibition coincides with the 250th anniversary and supplements the
> artist's longer material lineage.

Write the chain:

1. name the starting event and date;
2. calculate or state why the present year matters;
3. say who connected the exhibition to that anniversary;
4. explain what the additional works show.

Example pattern:

```text
《独立宣言》签署于 1776 年，2026 年正好是 250 周年。
主办方把这次展览放在这一时间背景下，希望观众重新思考国家、州界和土地之间的关系。
展厅还展示艺术家其他使用红土的作品，说明这种材料在他的创作中反复出现。
```

For relationships, name both sides and the connecting action. Do not say that
objects "form a structure" when the page can say they belong to one alliance,
oppose one another, share a material, or were shown together.

## 6. Integrate sources without interrupting reading

The public page should distinguish sources through link names and direct
attribution, not through repeated defensive prose.

Use:

- "FOR-SITE 介绍，这次展览……";
- "Galerie Lelong 的作品说明提到……";
- a linked date, venue, artwork title, or organizer name;
- a compact "继续阅读" group after the explanation.

Do not:

- repeat "according to the source" in every paragraph;
- expose internal labels such as direct fact, inference, or confidence beside
  each ordinary claim;
- repeat paragraphs explaining what the page is not claiming;
- ask the reader to compare multiple sources before understanding the basic
  object.

Maintain an internal research record when needed. If a real limitation belongs
on the page, summarize it once after the main explanation according to Section
2 rather than spreading it through the reading experience.

## 7. Name and implement clickable links

Every public source reference must be a real, clickable link. The visible text
must be a descriptive label, not the URL itself.

Use descriptive labels that summarize the destination:

- `FOR-SITE｜《Red Flags》旧金山展览信息`
- `Galerie Lelong｜作品起源与红土材料`
- `Fort Mason｜展馆与到访信息`

Do not use:

- raw URLs;
- "来源";
- "查看这里";
- "点击阅读";
- numeric footnote codes without a readable title;
- an organization name that gives no clue what the page contains.

Implement links with actual `<a href="…">` anchors or the framework's semantic
link component, keyboard focus, sufficient contrast, and an external-link
indication when they open another site. Do not render a URL as inert text.
Verify the final `href`, not only the visible label.

Link directly to the relevant article, event, collection, object, or official
record rather than a search result or general homepage.

In browser QA, click every changed source link and confirm that it:

- is keyboard reachable and visibly identifiable as a link;
- opens or navigates successfully;
- resolves to the intended destination rather than an error, blocked redirect,
  search page, or unrelated homepage;
- remains usable in the deployed preview, not only in local markup.

## 8. Keep analysis concrete

Do not turn every information page into critical-thinking training.

Begin with the visible object and its plain mechanism. Add interpretation only
when it helps the newcomer notice something specific.

Prefer:

- "普通州旗用图案区分各州；这组旗帜去掉图案，差别来自土壤本身的颜色。"

Avoid:

- "统一显出运输、劳动与策展所需的基础设施。"

The first statement tells the reader what to compare. The second introduces
several abstractions that the page has not shown.

For every analytical sentence ask:

- What visible detail supports it?
- Can the reader point to the compared objects?
- Does it answer a likely beginner question?
- Can it be rewritten as an ordinary observation?

If not, remove it or save it for an optional advanced essay.

Cards, figures, and interaction must also make complete explanatory claims:

- a card must do more than display a name and category; add the one sentence
  that explains why the person, place, term, work, or number belongs here;
- use timelines for order, maps for location, comparison views for difference,
  and diagrams for mechanisms or relationships;
- require every image to identify the subject, show a relevant form, support a
  comparison, or explain a place, process, or mechanism;
- write captions that add identity, date, role, context, or a useful visual
  observation rather than an obvious disclaimer;
- clearly label AI-generated images, modern reconstructions, imagined scenes,
  materially altered maps, and replicas when a reader could mistake them for
  primary evidence;
- do not label ordinary representations with redundant common-sense warnings;
- make interactive learning tasks use knowledge already taught on the page.
  The site may provide a complex tool, but it must not require the reader to
  become a programmer, researcher, or designer before using it.

## 9. Plain-language QA

Before approval and again in the rendered page, run:

### Evidence-boundary scan

Search for:

- 不能证明;
- 不能单独证明;
- 不是直接事实;
- 证据与边界;
- 来源边界;
- 结构性解读;
- 不等同于;
- 唯一结论.

Review every match. Remove repeated boilerplate and claim-by-claim defensive
language. Keep local qualifiers only when deleting them would make a claim
materially false. If a public evidence-boundary note is genuinely useful,
combine it into one closing note and confirm its length matches the importance
of the issue.

### Three-part scan

For short entries:

- count major explanatory headings;
- combine artificial Context, Why, Evidence, Analysis, and Boundary fragments;
- preserve only the clearest three-part learning sequence.

### Paraphrase test

Ask a newcomer to explain every paragraph in ordinary words. Rewrite any
sentence that requires knowing the writer's analytical vocabulary.

### Link test

Verify:

- every named source is clickable;
- the label explains the destination;
- a browser click opens the relevant page successfully;
- focus and contrast are visible;
- no raw URL, inert URL text, generic "source" label, error target, or
  misleading redirect remains.

### Meaning test

For each sentence, answer:

- What does this sentence mean?
- Why is it here?
- What does the next sentence add?

If the answer requires another explanation longer than the original sentence,
rewrite it.

### Object-and-process test

Ask whether a first-time reader can answer:

1. What or who is this?
2. Where and when does it belong?
3. What concretely happened or what can I see?
4. How does it work or unfold?
5. What example, result, or comparison supports the explanation?
6. Why is it worth attention?
7. What, if anything, remains disputed or limited?

Fail if the reader can repeat only an abstract theme such as "the issue is
complex" but cannot describe the object itself.

### Container and learning-task test

Verify:

- every card contains a useful conclusion, not only a noun and label;
- every image, chart, map, timeline, and diagram has an explanatory job;
- essential information is not hidden in tiny captions or collapsed content;
- each important number shows its origin or calculation when that relationship
  is necessary for understanding;
- an exercise or interactive task does not demand knowledge the page has not
  taught.

Record:

```text
CODEX_PLAIN_LANGUAGE_GATE: PASS | FAIL
```

## 10. Copy template

```markdown
# <Concrete subject title>

## 01 · What is it?
<Who, what, where, when, and the first orienting fact.>

## 02 · How does it work?
<Material, form, sequence, mechanism, or relationship in plain language.>

## 03 · Why is it worth attention?
<One concrete comparison, meaning, or question grounded in what the reader can see.>

### Continue reading
- [<Organization｜descriptive page name>](<direct URL>)
- [<Organization｜descriptive page name>](<direct URL>)

### Evidence and boundaries
<Optional. Include once, after the main explanation and links, only when a
meaningful dispute, uncertainty, missing record, reconstruction, or
interpretive limit needs explanation. Keep it brief unless that issue is itself
important content.>
```
