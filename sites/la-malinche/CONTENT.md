## Core argument

The site follows one woman through three names:

| Name | Historical / narrative role |
| --- | --- |
| Malintzin / 马林钦 | A Nahua noble girl whose birth name is uncertain, later sold into slavery |
| Doña Marina / 玛丽娜夫人 | A baptized translator and diplomatic actor inside the Spanish-Indigenous coalition |
| La Malinche / 拉·马琳切 | A later national symbol made to carry betrayal, origin, gender, and colonial trauma |

The site must not flatten her into either saviour or traitor. It ends with the manuscript’s position: she helped accelerate conquest and its violence, while history also suppressed her voice and compressed an era’s responsibility into one woman.

## Evidence boundaries

### Historical fact

- The manuscript places her birth around 1500 and stresses that the date and birth name are unknown.
- Spanish accounts describe noble origin; the manuscript treats elite Nahuatl speech as additional evidence.
- She learned Maya languages after being sold away from her home region.
- She was among twenty women given to the Spaniards after the battle at Potonchán.
- She became central to translation and diplomacy during the campaign.
- Coalition warfare, epidemic disease, siege, hunger, and Spanish military technology all shaped Tenochtitlan’s fall.

### Scholarly interpretation

- Whether family inheritance, political tribute, or another process caused her enslavement.
- The degree of agency available under repeated captivity and coercion.
- Her role in warnings associated with Cholula and the contested scale and cause of the massacre.
- Her feelings toward Moctezuma, Cortés, the fall of Tenochtitlan, and later family arrangements.
- The date and cause of her death.
- How Indigenous memory, nationalist history, and feminist reinterpretation should be weighed.

### Narrative framing

- “One woman, three names.”
- Language as both survival method and instrument of conquest.
- “Using her own voice to end an era.”
- The question “Who betrayed the betrayer?”

### Visual metaphor

- Creator cover images are modern editorial artwork, not historical portraits.
- Creator maps explain the manuscript’s geography; they are not contemporary sixteenth-century maps.
- CSS codex pattern, lake strata, broken rules, and red strike-throughs are modern interpretive graphics.

## Required qualifications

Do not remove wording equivalent to:

- `大约`, `可能`, `或许`, `据说`, `无人知晓`, `众说纷纭`, `史料互相冲突`.
- A first-person emotional reconstruction is narrative framing, not a recovered diary.
- “Aztec Empire,” “Nahua,” “Mexica,” and later Mexican national identity are not interchangeable.
- Applying nineteenth-century nationalism to a sixteenth-century city-state world risks anachronism.
- Indigenous allies were political actors; the conquest was not a two-party Spain-versus-Indigenous story.
- Malinche’s participation does not remove Spanish colonial violence or make later suffering her individual responsibility.

## Protected conclusion

The ending must remain morally double:

- In outcome, she helped the conquest.
- From her position, she had already been repeatedly betrayed and worked to survive.
- Later history erased her contribution and made her carry a collective wound alone.
- She is neither a simple saviour nor a timeless traitor, but a person inside coercive historical forces.
